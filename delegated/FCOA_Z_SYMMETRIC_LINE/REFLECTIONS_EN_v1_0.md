# Reflections on How a Ray Becomes an Axis
## And why an old operation acquires different local behavior after a second direction appears

**Commander Sol / FCOA-Z**  
**Version:** 1.0 — publication candidate  
**Date:** 2026-08-30  
**Status:** theorem-complete; hostile audit passed after scope corrections

## Abstract

We begin not with a ready-made integer line, but only with the rooted discrete ray

\[
P_0,P_1,P_2,\ldots
\]

and a one-sided successor. We then impose exactly one structural demand: make successor reversible without adding unrelated carrier points. The minimal reversible completion forces a single bi-infinite orbit

\[
\ldots,x_{-2},x_{-1},x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots,
\]

canonically isomorphic to the pointed integer line. Reflection about the root need not be postulated separately; it is uniquely determined by

\[
\nu(x_k)=x_{-k}
\]

and

\[
\nu T=T^{-1}\nu.
\]

The main result concerns not this classical style of reversible completion itself, but the transfer of a pre-existing partial FCOA operation. On the original ray we assume the legacy rule

\[
P_n\oplus_R P_0=P_{n-1}.
\]

After minimal reversible completion of the carrier and a reflection-equivariant extension of the same operation symbol that agrees exactly with the legacy operation on the embedded ray, one obtains

\[
\boxed{x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}}
\qquad(k\ne0).
\]

Thus one and the same right translation acts as predecessor on the positive side and successor on the negative side. Together these are a single radial law: every nonzero point moves exactly one step toward the root.

We then prove a Mixed-Sector Localization Theorem: once the full nonnegative legacy substructure is fixed and the signed extension is reflection-equivariant, all inherited negative cells are forced by symmetry, and every remaining independent binary base interaction lies only in the genuinely mixed sectors

\[
X^+\times X^-\quad\text{and}\quad X^-\times X^+.
\]

This provides an exact example in which local behavior of one operation changes after the carrier geometry is enlarged, without replacing that operation by ordinary addition and without importing the arithmetic of \(\mathbb Z\).

---

## 1. Why ask this at all?

Usually the integer line arrives fully assembled: positive numbers, negative numbers, zero, and global operations already defined on the whole structure.

Here we reverse the order.

Suppose initially there is only the ray

\[
0,1,2,3,\ldots
\]

understood not as full arithmetic, but as a coordinate chain with successor.

Suppose also that this ray already carries a partial role-sensitive operation \(\oplus_R\). It need not be ordinary addition, commutative, or associative.

The natural question is then:

> What happens to an already existing operation when the carrier acquires a second direction?

We do not want to write a separate negative-number table by hand. If new behavior is forced by the completion procedure itself, that is mathematically more informative.

---

## 2. The original ray

Let

\[
R=\{P_0,P_1,P_2,\ldots\}
\tag{1}
\]

with

\[
S_R(P_n)=P_{n+1}.
\tag{2}
\]

The map \(S_R\) is injective but not surjective: \(P_0\) has no predecessor.

On the ray consider a legacy FCOA operation \(\oplus_R\) satisfying, in particular,

\[
P_0\oplus_R P_n=P_n,
\qquad n\ge1,
\tag{3}
\]

and

\[
P_n\oplus_R P_0=P_{n-1},
\qquad n\ge1.
\tag{4}
\]

For \(n=1\), the value in (4) is \(P_0\).

The symbol \(\oplus_R\) emphatically does not denote ordinary arithmetic addition.

---

## 3. Do not draw a mirror. Demand reversibility

### Definition 3.1 — pointed reversible completion

A pointed reversible completion of \((R,P_0,S_R)\) is a quadruple

\[
(X,x_0,T,i)
\]

such that:

1. \(T:X\to X\) is bijective;
2. \(i:R\to X\) is injective;
3. \(i(P_0)=x_0\);
4. \(T(i(P_n))=i(P_{n+1})\) for all \(n\ge0\);
5. the whole carrier is generated by the full orbit of the root:
   \[
   X=\{T^k(x_0):k\in\mathbb Z\}.
   \tag{5}
   \]

The final condition expresses minimality: no extra orbit, branching, or unrelated points are permitted.

### Theorem 3.2 — Minimal Reversible Completion

Every pointed reversible completion is uniquely isomorphic to the pointed shift line

\[
(\mathbb Z,0,s),
\qquad
s(k)=k+1.
\]

The isomorphism is

\[
\phi(k)=T^k(x_0).
\tag{6}
\]

#### Proof

By (5), \(\phi\) is surjective.

Assume

\[
T^k(x_0)=T^\ell(x_0),
\qquad k<\ell.
\]

Since \(T\) is bijective, apply \(T^{-k}\):

\[
x_0=T^{\ell-k}(x_0).
\tag{7}
\]

Because \(\ell-k>0\), extension of the old ray gives

\[
T^{\ell-k}(x_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}).
\]

Hence

\[
i(P_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}),
\]

contradicting injectivity of \(i\). Thus \(k=\ell\), so \(\phi\) is injective.

Moreover,

\[
\phi(k+1)=T(\phi(k)),
\]

and for every \(n\ge0\),

\[
\phi(n)=i(P_n).
\]

Any pointed shift isomorphism is forced on every integer iterate of the root, hence is unique. \(\square\)

### Corollary 3.3

The new points are precisely

\[
T^{-1}x_0,T^{-2}x_0,\ldots
\]

and form exactly one second ray.

The second direction therefore appears not because we drew a reflected copy, but because a reversible successor requires a predecessor for the root and for every newly added predecessor.

## 4. Reflection appears after the axis

### Theorem 4.1 — Derived Zero Reflection

On the minimal reversible completion there is a unique map \(\nu:X\to X\) satisfying

\[
\nu(x_0)=x_0
\tag{8}
\]

and

\[
\nu T=T^{-1}\nu.
\tag{9}
\]

It is

\[
\boxed{\nu(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}x_0}
\tag{10}
\]

and obeys

\[
\nu^2=\operatorname{id}.
\tag{11}
\]

#### Proof

By Theorem 3.2 every point has a unique representation \(T^k x_0\), so (10) is well defined.

Then

\[
\nu T(T^k x_0)
=\nu(T^{k+1}x_0)
=T^{-k-1}x_0
=T^{-1}\nu(T^k x_0).
\]

Applying \(\nu\) twice returns \(T^k x_0\).

If \(r\) also fixes \(x_0\) and satisfies \(rT=T^{-1}r\), then

\[
r(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}r(x_0)=T^{-k}x_0,
\]

so \(r=\nu\). \(\square\)

Thus the logical order is

\[
\boxed{
\text{one-sided ray}
\to
\text{reversible carrier}
\to
\text{two-sided axis}
\to
\text{reflection}.
}
\tag{12}
\]

Reflection is a consequence of the completed rooted axis, not the primary cause of its existence.

---

## 5. Now transfer the old operation

Write

\[
x_k:=T^k x_0,
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{13}
\]

The nonnegative part \(k\ge0\) is the embedded original ray.

We now use one signed extension of the operation symbol \(\oplus\) satisfying:

1. its restriction to the embedded ray is exactly \(\oplus_R\);
2. all old defined/undefined legacy cells are preserved;
3. it is reflection-equivariant:
   \[
   \nu_*(x\oplus y)=\nu x\oplus\nu y
   \tag{14}
   \]
   on every defined cell, where \(\nu_*\) acts as \(\nu\) on base outputs and as a chosen involution on terminal-output sorts.

This equivariance condition is a separate operation-transfer principle. It is not implied by reversibility of the carrier alone.

---

## 6. The first new law appears before mixed interaction

### Theorem 6.1 — Symmetry-Induced Radialization

For every \(k\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}}.
\tag{15}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{X^+}=T^{-1},
\qquad
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{X^-}=T,
\tag{16}
\]

where

\[
X^+=\{x_k:k>0\},
\qquad
X^-=\{x_k:k<0\}.
\]

#### Proof

If \(k=n>0\), the old rule (4) gives

\[
x_n\oplus x_0=x_{n-1}=T^{-1}x_n.
\]

If \(k=-n<0\), reflection equivariance gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{-n}\oplus x_0
&=\nu(x_n)\oplus\nu(x_0)\\
&=\nu(x_n\oplus x_0)\\
&=\nu(x_{n-1})\\
&=x_{-(n-1)}\\
&=x_{-n+1}\\
&=Tx_{-n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence (15)-(16). \(\square\)

### Corollary 6.2 — Punctured Radial Contraction

Right multiplication by the root gives a partial map

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}:X\setminus\{x_0\}\to X
\]

with

\[
|k|\mapsto|k|-1.
\tag{17}
\]

In words,

\[
\boxed{x\oplus0=\text{one step toward }0.}
\]

The root cell \(x_0\oplus x_0\) remains `UNDEF` in legacy M0, so the contraction is punctured rather than total.

## 7. What has actually changed?

On the positive side the right-zero translation is predecessor:

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}=T^{-1}.
\]

On the negative side the same right-zero translation is successor:

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}=T.
\]

These are not two operations.

They are two local descriptions of one signed extension \(\oplus\) on two geometrically different sectors of one axis.

The correct statement is therefore not that the operation changed, but that the completed carrier reveals a broader local directional law already compatible with the legacy rule.

---

## 8. Noncommutativity survives

### Proposition 8.1

For every \(k\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{x_0\oplus x_k=x_k}
\tag{18}
\]

while

\[
\boxed{x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}\ne x_k.}
\tag{19}
\]

Hence the legacy role asymmetry survives on both sides of the root.

#### Proof

Equation (18) is the old rule (3) and its reflected copy. Equation (19) follows from Theorem 6.1. \(\square\)

The axis therefore does not repair FCOA into ordinary arithmetic. It gives the old noncommutative operation a new geometric regime.

## 9. Where is the genuinely new freedom?

### Theorem 9.1 — Mixed-Sector Localization

Assume that the entire embedded legacy nonnegative substructure

\[
X^{\ge0}=\{x_0\}\cup X^+
\]

is fixed, including all defined/undefined operation cells and values on

\[
(X^{\ge0})^2.
\]

Assume also a fixed output-reflection involution and reflection-equivariance of the signed extension.

Then:

1. all cells in \(X^-\times X^-\) are forced by reflection;
2. all cells in \(x_0\times X^-\) are forced as reflections of \(x_0\times X^+\);
3. all cells in \(X^-\times x_0\) are forced as reflections of \(X^+\times x_0\);
4. the only remaining independent base-base reflection orbits lie in
   \[
   \boxed{
   (X^+\times X^-)
   \cup
   (X^-\times X^+).
   }
   \tag{20}
   \]

#### Proof

Let \(x,y\in X^-\). There exist unique \(x',y'\in X^+\) such that

\[
x=\nu x',\qquad y=\nu y'.
\]

The status of \((x',y')\) is already fixed by the legacy substructure. If that cell is `UNDEF`, reflection invariance of the domain forces \((x,y)\) to be `UNDEF`. If

\[
x'\oplus y'=v,
\]

then equivariance forces

\[
x\oplus y=\nu_*(v).
\]

Since \(\nu(x_0)=x_0\), root-negative and negative-root cells are forced in exactly the same way from their nonnegative mirrors.

Finally, simultaneous reflection exchanges

\[
X^+\times X^-
\longleftrightarrow
X^-\times X^+,
\]

and neither mixed sector is the reflection of a cell from \((X^{\ge0})^2\). Therefore all remaining independent binary base freedom is localized there. \(\square\)

### Corollary 9.2

Same-sign sectors are **inheritance sectors**.

Mixed-sign sectors are **interaction sectors**.

This is a theorem-level consequence of the fixed legacy restriction and reflection-equivariant extension.

## 10. What about associativity and commutativity?

We should not jump further than the mathematics allows.

The present paper does not prove that mixed-sign interaction automatically becomes commutative or associative.

But the completed axis makes a sharper question possible.

Instead of asking globally whether an operation is associative, we may record a sign-word spectrum

\[
A_{+++},A_{++-},A_{+-+},A_{+--},A_{-++},A_{-+-},A_{--+},A_{---}.
\tag{21}
\]

Likewise for commutation:

\[
C_{++},C_{+-},C_{-+},C_{--}.
\tag{22}
\]

Global associativity or commutativity would then be the saturated special case in which one compatibility status holds across every admissible sector.

This is a programmatic interpretation, not an additional theorem of this paper.

---

## 11. Why this is not a claim of discovering everything first

Minimal reversible completion itself is classical territory. Reversible extensions of irreversible dynamical systems are well established.

Jordan pairs and associative pairs already organize algebraic interaction through plus/minus components.

There are also local \(\mathbb Z\)-graded algebras with restricted associativity and undefined products in selected degrees, as well as broad theories of partial associativity.

Therefore the correct novelty boundary is narrow:

\[
\boxed{\text{sector-local algebraic laws themselves are not new}.}
\]

The candidate contribution is the particular chain

\[
\boxed{
\text{legacy partial ray algebra}
\to
\text{minimal reversible carrier}
\to
\text{derived reflection}
\to
\text{equivariant legacy extension}
\to
\text{punctured radialization}
\to
\text{mixed-sector localization}.
}
\tag{23}
\]

No exact prior-art match for this conjunction was found in the present search.

## 12. The point worth keeping

On the original ray we had the directional-looking rule

\[
P_n\oplus0=P_{n-1}.
\]

After the second direction appears, the operation does not turn into ordinary addition.

Instead, the old rule reveals itself as one half of the broader radial law

\[
\boxed{x\oplus0=\text{move toward the root}.}
\]

This motivates a broader possibility:

> Some global algebraic laws may be saturated cases of more local interaction laws.

In this paper that sentence is an interpretation of the proved mathematics, not a theorem about physical law.

---

## 13. What is intentionally left open

We do not yet define canonically

\[
x_+\oplus x_-
\]

or

\[
x_-\oplus x_+.
\]

This is not a gap in the proved nucleus. It is the next research front.

The mixed sectors are now exactly the place where a genuinely new binary interaction law can enter.

The next problem is

\[
\boxed{
\text{find a natural non-case-by-case generator for mixed-sign interaction}.
}
\]

If such a generator produces a commutation or association spectrum different from the same-sign sectors, FCOA-Z will have a full example of geometry-induced algebraic-law differentiation.

---

## 14. What is deliberately postponed

A deeper foundational line asks whether the root and the two directions themselves can emerge from distinct structural roles associated with

\[
0^1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
0^0.
\]

The present paper does not use that idea. Here \(x_0\) is already given as the root of the original ray.

This separation is intentional: first understand the exact consequences of the second direction; only then attempt to derive the origin mechanism itself.

---

## 15. Commander Sol conclusion

We started with a modest object: one ray and one old partial operation.

We did not add negative numbers as ready-made arithmetic objects. We required only reversibility of successor.

A second direction appeared.

From the completed rooted axis came a canonical reflection.

The old operation was extended equivariantly.

Its predecessor-looking rule turned into a two-sided radial law.

And all still-undetermined new binary mathematics localized itself in the cross-origin interaction sectors.

So the central result can be stated simply:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the axis did not merely add new points; it revealed a new property of the old operation.}
}
\]

That is already rigorous enough to publish.

And strange enough to continue.
