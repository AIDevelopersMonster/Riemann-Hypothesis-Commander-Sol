# From a Numerical Ray to a Two-Sided Axis: Symmetry-Induced Local Laws in FCOA

**Version:** 0.1 — prepublication draft  
**Date:** 2026-08-30  
**Status:** theorem-complete draft nucleus; external hostile audit required before publication

## Abstract

We study a Fixed-Carrier Oriented Algebra (FCOA) initially defined on the rooted discrete ray

\[
P_0,P_1,P_2,\ldots
\]

with a fixed successor and role-sensitive partial operations. We do not import the arithmetic structure of \(\mathbb Z\). Instead we form the minimal reversible completion of the successor action: the one-sided injective shift is extended to a bijective shift, and minimality forces a unique bi-infinite orbit canonically isomorphic to the pointed integer line. Reflection about the root is then derived as the unique involution conjugating successor with predecessor.

The main result concerns not this classical style of reversible completion itself, but the transfer of a pre-existing FCOA operation. The legacy rule

\[
P_n\oplus P_0=P_{n-1}
\]

becomes, after reversible completion and reflection-equivariant transfer,

\[
x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}.
\]

Thus the same operation with the same right argument acts as predecessor on the positive half-line and successor on the negative half-line. The local directional law is not introduced by a sign case table; it is forced by carrier geometry, reversibility, and symmetry. We also prove a Mixed-Sector Localization Theorem: once the positive legacy sector is fixed and the negative sector is generated by reflection, all remaining binary base-operation freedom is confined to opposite-sign sectors.

This provides an exact example in which one fixed operation on one fixed carrier acquires different local interaction laws from the geometry of the completed carrier. No physical claim is made.

## 1. Introduction

Standard algebra often begins with global axioms: an operation is declared commutative, associative, distributive, and so on. FCOA takes the opposite direction. Operations are partial, typed, and role-sensitive, and one then asks which laws actually hold on which parts of the carrier.

The goal of this paper is to examine the passage from a one-sided coordinate ray to a two-sided numerical axis and to determine whether new properties of an already existing operation can emerge without replacing that operation.

We separate two levels carefully:

1. minimal reversible extension of a one-sided shift to a two-sided shift is a classical type of construction;
2. the induced behavior of a particular legacy FCOA operation under that completion is the object of interest here.

Any novelty claim must therefore concern only the second level and its combination with partiality, typed outputs, automorphism memory, and mixed-sector localization.

## 2. The rooted ray

Let

\[
R=\{P_0,P_1,P_2,\ldots\}
\tag{1}
\]

and

\[
S_R(P_n)=P_{n+1}.
\tag{2}
\]

The map \(S_R\) is injective but not surjective because \(P_0\) has no predecessor.

On this ray we consider a legacy FCOA operation \(\oplus\) satisfying in particular

\[
P_0\oplus P_n=P_n \qquad(n\ge1),
\tag{3}
\]

\[
P_n\oplus P_0=P_{n-1}\qquad(n\ge1),
\tag{4}
\]

with \(P_0\) obtained when \(n=1\). Additional diagonal terminal-output rules are part of the full M0 background but are not needed for the main theorem below.

The symbol \(\oplus\) does not denote ordinary addition.

---

## 3. Minimal reversible completion

### Definition 3.1

A pointed reversible completion of \((R,P_0,S_R)\) is a quadruple

\[
(X,x_0,T,i)
\]

such that:

1. \(T:X\to X\) is bijective;
2. \(i:R\to X\) is injective;
3. \(i(P_0)=x_0\);
4. \(T(i(P_n))=i(P_{n+1})\) for all \(n\ge0\);
5. the carrier is generated by the full orbit of the root:
   \[
   X=\{T^k(x_0):k\in\mathbb Z\}.
   \tag{5}
   \]

Condition (5) excludes unrelated extra points and expresses minimality.

### Theorem 3.2 — Minimal Reversible Completion

Every pointed reversible completion is uniquely isomorphic to

\[
(\mathbb Z,0,s),\qquad s(k)=k+1.
\]

The isomorphism is

\[
\phi(k)=T^k(x_0).
\tag{6}
\]

#### Proof

By (5), \(\phi\) is surjective. Suppose

\[
T^k(x_0)=T^\ell(x_0),\qquad k<\ell.
\]

Since \(T\) is bijective, apply \(T^{-k}\):

\[
x_0=T^{\ell-k}(x_0).
\]

Because \(\ell-k>0\), the extension property gives

\[
T^{\ell-k}(x_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}).
\]

Thus

\[
i(P_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}),
\]

contradicting injectivity of \(i\). Therefore \(k=\ell\), so \(\phi\) is injective.

Moreover,

\[
\phi(k+1)=T^{k+1}(x_0)=T(\phi(k)),
\]

and for \(n\ge0\),

\[
\phi(n)=T^n(x_0)=i(P_n).
\]

Any pointed shift isomorphism is forced on every iterate of the root, so it is unique. \(\square\)

### Corollary 3.3

The new points are precisely

\[
T^{-1}x_0,T^{-2}x_0,\ldots
\]

and form one left ray attached to the original root. Branching and wrap-around are excluded by minimality.

## 4. Reflection derived from reversibility

### Theorem 4.1 — Derived Reflection

There exists a unique map \(\nu:X\to X\) satisfying

\[
\nu(x_0)=x_0
\tag{7}
\]

and

\[
\nu T=T^{-1}\nu.
\tag{8}
\]

It is

\[
\nu(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}x_0
\tag{9}
\]

and obeys

\[
\nu^2=\operatorname{id}.
\tag{10}
\]

#### Proof

By Theorem 3.2 every point has a unique form \(T^k x_0\), so (9) is well defined. Then

\[
\nu T(T^k x_0)=T^{-(k+1)}x_0=T^{-1}\nu(T^k x_0),
\]

and applying (9) twice returns \(T^k x_0\).

If \(r\) also satisfies (7)-(8), then

\[
r(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}r(x_0)=T^{-k}x_0,
\]

so \(r=\nu\). \(\square\)

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\text{reversibility}\to\text{two-sided line}\to\text{reflection}.
}
\tag{11}
\]

## 5. Transfer of the legacy operation

Write

\[
x_k:=T^k x_0,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
\tag{12}
\]

The positive part \(k\ge0\) is the embedded original ray.

Extend \(\oplus\) minimally while preserving all old positive-sector cells and imposing simultaneous reflection equivariance:

\[
\nu(x\oplus y)=\nu x\oplus\nu y
\tag{13}
\]

for base-valued cells, with the corresponding involution on terminal output sorts.

---

## 6. Symmetry-Induced Radialization

### Theorem 6.1

For every \(k\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{
x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}.}
\tag{14}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{k>0}=T^{-1},
\qquad
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{k<0}=T.
\tag{15}
\]

Thus the same right translation of the same operation acts as predecessor on the positive half-line and successor on the negative half-line.

#### Proof

For \(k=n>0\), the old rule (4) gives

\[
x_n\oplus x_0=x_{n-1}=T^{-1}x_n.
\]

For \(k=-n<0\), reflection equivariance gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{-n}\oplus x_0
&=\nu(x_n)\oplus\nu(x_0)\\
&=\nu(x_n\oplus x_0)\\
&=\nu(x_{n-1})\\
&=x_{-(n-1)}\\
&=x_{-n+1}=Tx_{-n}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence (14)-(15). \(\square\)

### Corollary 6.2 — Radial form

Right multiplication by the root reduces absolute coordinate depth by one:

\[
|k|\mapsto |k|-1.
\tag{16}
\]

Thus a one-sided directional law becomes a radial law after reversible completion.

---

## 7. The operation has not been replaced

### Proposition 7.1

For every \(k\ne0\),

\[
x_0\oplus x_k=x_k,
\tag{17}
\]

whereas

\[
x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}\ne x_k.
\tag{18}
\]

Hence the original role asymmetry and noncommutativity survive on both sides of the completed line.

#### Proof

Equation (17) is the old left-zero rule and its reflected copy. Equation (18) follows from Theorem 6.1. \(\square\)

The new local directional law is therefore not produced by replacing \(\oplus\) with ordinary addition.

## 8. Localization of new binary freedom

Let

\[
X^+=\{x_k:k>0\},
\qquad
X^-=\{x_k:k<0\}.
\]

### Theorem 8.1 — Mixed-Sector Localization

If the positive legacy sector is fixed and the extension is reflection-equivariant, then the whole negative-negative sector is forced. Every remaining new base-base domain/value choice lies in

\[
\boxed{
(X^+\times X^-)
\cup
(X^-\times X^+).
}
\tag{19}
\]

#### Proof

For any \(x,y\in X^-\), there are unique \(x',y'\in X^+\) with

\[
x=\nu x',\qquad y=\nu y'.
\]

If \((x',y')\) is undefined, reflection invariance of the domain forces \((x,y)\) to be undefined. If

\[
x'\oplus y'=v,
\]

then equivariance forces

\[
x\oplus y=\nu_*(v).
\]

Thus there is no free choice in the negative-negative sector. Mixed-sign pairs are not reflections of positive-positive pairs, so they remain the only unconstrained binary base orbits. \(\square\)

### Corollary 8.2

Same-sign sectors are inheritance sectors; mixed-sign sectors are the unique sectors of new binary interaction freedom.

## 9. Local laws rather than global axioms

For a partial FCOA operation, a subtraction-based associator is not always well typed. We therefore use the association status

\[
\mathcal A_\oplus(x,y,z)
\in\{EQ,NEQ,LEFT,RIGHT,NONE\}.
\]

Likewise, commutation is analyzed through definedness and equality of the two directions.

On the signed line this naturally yields sector spectra

\[
\mathcal C_{++},\mathcal C_{+-},\mathcal C_{-+},\mathcal C_{--}
\tag{20}
\]

and

\[
\mathcal A_{+++},\mathcal A_{++-},\ldots,\mathcal A_{---}.
\tag{21}
\]

Global commutativity or associativity can then be viewed as the saturated special case in which the same compatibility status holds throughout all admissible sectors.

This paper does not yet choose a canonical mixed-sector value law. It therefore does not claim that a particular mixed sector is already commutative or associative. The proven earlier fact is that carrier completion alone creates different local directional behavior for one operation and localizes all further new binary freedom to the mixed sectors.

## 10. Relation to prior frameworks

Related ideas appear in several mature theories. Jordan pairs use two components \(V^+,V^-\) with products of sign pattern

\[
V^\sigma\times V^{-\sigma}\times V^\sigma\to V^\sigma.
\]

Associative pairs likewise organize identities by alternating signs. Graded, super, and color algebras make exchange laws depend on grades. Categories and groupoids provide partial composition whose associativity is meaningful only on composable triples.

In local/algebraic quantum field theory, commutativity of observables may depend on geometric separation: spacelike-separated observables commute even though the full operator algebra need not be commutative.

These precedents show that sector-local and geometry-local algebraic laws are mathematically serious. They do not establish a physical interpretation of FCOA-Z.

## 11. What is and is not proved

### Proved

1. minimal reversible completion of the rooted successor ray is one bi-infinite orbit;
2. it is canonically isomorphic to the pointed integer line;
3. root reflection is uniquely derived from reversible shift;
4. the legacy \(\oplus\) is preserved on the positive ray;
5. its right-zero action becomes radial contraction;
6. the local direction of the same operation is opposite on the two sides;
7. new binary freedom is localized to mixed-sign sectors;
8. the original positional noncommutativity survives.

### Not proved and not claimed

- a canonical mixed-sector value law;
- recovery of ordinary signed addition from mixed interactions;
- that commutativity or associativity are physically non-fundamental;
- novelty of reversible completion itself;
- a physical model of real space.

## 12. Main conclusion

The proved chain is

\[
\boxed{
\text{rooted ray}
\to
\text{minimal reversible completion}
\to
\text{derived reflection}
\to
\text{legacy-operation transfer}
\to
\text{radialization}
\to
\text{mixed-sector localization}.
}
\tag{22}
\]

It gives an explicit mechanism in which one partial operation on one carrier acquires different local directional laws not by replacing the operation and not by importing ordinary arithmetic, but as a consequence of the completed carrier geometry.

This motivates the broader research hypothesis

\[
\boxed{
\text{some global algebraic laws may be saturated cases of local interaction laws.}
}
\tag{23}
\]

Equation (23) is a programmatic interpretation of the proved mathematics, not a claim about fundamental physical law.

The next problem is deliberately kept outside the present theorem nucleus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{find a natural non-case-by-case generator for mixed-sign interaction}
}
\]

such that the commutation and association spectra provably differ between same-sign and mixed-sign sectors without importing ordinary addition or multiplication.

That step will determine whether radialization is the first instance of a broader theory of geometry-induced algebraic law differentiation.
