## 1. Purpose

This note replaces the purely pictorial phrase “add a mirror ray to the left of zero” by a stronger structural construction.

The starting carrier is the rooted ray

\[
R=\{P_0,P_1,P_2,\ldots\}
\]

with successor

\[
S_R(P_n)=P_{n+1}.
\]

The map \(S_R\) is injective but not surjective: \(P_0\) has no predecessor.

The goal is to adjoin the **least amount of new carrier needed to make successor reversible**.

The result is a bi-infinite pointed line canonically isomorphic to \(\mathbb Z\). Reflection is then derived from reversibility and the fixed origin; it need not be postulated as the primary construction.

---

## 2. Reversible completions

### Definition 2.1 — pointed reversible completion

A pointed reversible completion of \((R,P_0,S_R)\) is a quadruple

\[
(X,x_0,T,i)
\]

such that:

1. \(T:X\to X\) is a bijection;
2. \(i:R\to X\) is injective;
3. \(i(P_0)=x_0\);
4. for every \(n\ge0\),
   \[
   T(i(P_n))=i(P_{n+1});
   \tag{1}
   \]
5. the completion is generated by the orbit of the root:
   \[
   X=\{T^k(x_0):k\in\mathbb Z\}.
   \tag{2}
   \]

Condition (2) is the minimality condition: no unrelated extra carrier points are allowed.

## 3. Minimal Reversible Completion Theorem

### Theorem 3.1

Every pointed reversible completion \((X,x_0,T,i)\) of the rooted successor ray is uniquely isomorphic to the pointed shift line

\[
(\mathbb Z,0,s),
\qquad
s(k)=k+1.
\]

More precisely, the map

\[
\phi:\mathbb Z\to X,
\qquad
\phi(k)=T^k(x_0)
\tag{3}
\]

is a bijection satisfying

\[
\phi(0)=x_0,
\qquad
\phi(k+1)=T(\phi(k)),
\tag{4}
\]

and for every \(n\ge0\),

\[
\phi(n)=i(P_n).
\tag{5}
\]

Hence the minimal reversible completion is unique up to unique pointed shift isomorphism.

### Proof

By (2), every element of \(X\) is \(T^k(x_0)\) for some \(k\in\mathbb Z\), so \(\phi\) is surjective.

We prove injectivity. Suppose

\[
T^k(x_0)=T^\ell(x_0)
\]

with \(k<\ell\). Since \(T\) is bijective, applying \(T^{-k}\) gives

\[
x_0=T^{\ell-k}(x_0).
\tag{6}
\]

Because \(\ell-k>0\), repeated use of (1) and \(i(P_0)=x_0\) yields

\[
T^{\ell-k}(x_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}).
\]

Thus (6) gives

\[
i(P_0)=i(P_{\ell-k}),
\]

contradicting injectivity of \(i\), since \(\ell-k>0\). Therefore \(k=\ell\), and \(\phi\) is injective.

Equation (4) is immediate:

\[
\phi(k+1)=T^{k+1}(x_0)=T(T^k(x_0))=T(\phi(k)).
\]

For \(n\ge0\), induction using (1) gives

\[
T^n(x_0)=T^n(i(P_0))=i(P_n),
\]

which proves (5).

Uniqueness follows because any pointed shift isomorphism must send the root to the root and therefore must send the \(k\)-th iterate of the root to the \(k\)-th iterate. \(\square\)

## 4. Second direction is forced by reversibility

### Corollary 4.1

In the minimal reversible completion, the new points are exactly

\[
T^{-1}(x_0),T^{-2}(x_0),\ldots
\]

and form one left ray attached to the original root.

No branching, wrap-around, or additional orbit is compatible with the minimality requirement (2).

### Proof

Theorem 3.1 identifies all points with unique integer iterates \(T^k(x_0)\). The old ray is precisely \(k\ge0\); the genuinely new points are precisely \(k<0\). \(\square\)

Thus the passage

\[
\boxed{
\text{one-sided successor}
\longrightarrow
\text{minimal reversible successor}
}
\]

already produces the full two-sided coordinate line.

## 5. Reflection is derived, not primitive

### Theorem 5.1 — derived zero reflection

On every minimal reversible completion there is exactly one map

\[
\nu:X\to X
\]

such that

\[
\nu(x_0)=x_0
\tag{7}
\]

and

\[
\nu T=T^{-1}\nu.
\tag{8}
\]

It is given by

\[
\boxed{
\nu(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}x_0.
}
\tag{9}
\]

Moreover,

\[
\nu^2=\operatorname{id}_X.
\tag{10}
\]

### Proof

Because every point has a unique representation \(T^k x_0\), formula (9) is well defined. It fixes \(x_0\), and

\[
\nu T(T^k x_0)
=\nu(T^{k+1}x_0)
=T^{-(k+1)}x_0
=T^{-1}(T^{-k}x_0)
=T^{-1}\nu(T^k x_0),
\]

so (8) holds.

Applying (9) twice gives

\[
\nu^2(T^k x_0)=\nu(T^{-k}x_0)=T^k x_0,
\]

so (10) holds.

For uniqueness, if \(r\) satisfies (7)-(8), then for every \(k\in\mathbb Z\), repeated conjugation gives

\[
r(T^k x_0)=T^{-k}r(x_0)=T^{-k}x_0.
\]

Hence \(r=\nu\). \(\square\)

### Interpretation

The reflection symmetry is not required to create the second direction. It is a theorem of the minimal reversible line once the origin is fixed.

This strengthens the earlier mirror-copy construction:

\[
\boxed{
\text{reversibility first}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{two-sided line}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{reflection}.
}
\]

## 6. Order induced by the reversible orbit

Define

\[
T^k x_0<T^\ell x_0
\iff
k<\ell.
\tag{11}
\]

### Proposition 6.1

Relation (11) is a discrete linear order of type \(\mathbb Z\), with \(T\) as its immediate-successor map and \(T^{-1}\) as its immediate-predecessor map.

### Proof

Transport the standard order on \(\mathbb Z\) through the bijection \(\phi\) of Theorem 3.1. \(\square\)

This order is a coordinate structure derived from the orbit indexing. It does not introduce ordinary binary addition or multiplication into the FCOA operation signature.

## 7. Transfer of the legacy FCOA operation

Let \(\oplus_R\) be the original ray operation with the fixed zero rules

\[
P_0\oplus_R P_n=P_n
\qquad(n\ge1),
\tag{12}
\]

\[
P_n\oplus_R P_0=P_{n-1}
\qquad(n\ge1),
\tag{13}
\]

where \(P_{0}\) is the result when \(n=1\).

Extend the operation minimally to the reversible line subject to simultaneous reflection equivariance, as established in `SIGNED_M0_REFLECTION_TRANSFER_0_1.md`.

Write

\[
x_k:=T^k x_0
\qquad(k\in\mathbb Z).
\]

Thus \(x_k\) corresponds externally to integer coordinate \(k\).

## 8. Symmetry-Induced Radialization Theorem

### Theorem 8.1

For every \(k\ne0\), the same right-zero translation of the extended legacy operation satisfies

\[
\boxed{
x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}.}
\tag{14}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{k>0}=T^{-1},
\qquad
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}\big|_{k<0}=T.
}
\tag{15}
\]

Thus right multiplication by the same origin element acts as global predecessor on the positive side and global successor on the negative side.

In both cases it moves exactly one step toward the root.

### Proof

For \(k=n>0\), (13) gives

\[
x_n\oplus x_0=x_{n-1}=T^{-1}(x_n).
\]

For \(k=-n<0\), reflection equivariance gives

\[
x_{-n}\oplus x_0
=\nu(x_n)\oplus\nu(x_0)
=\nu(x_n\oplus x_0)
=\nu(x_{n-1})
=x_{-(n-1)}
=x_{-n+1}
=T(x_{-n}).
\]

This is exactly (14)-(15). \(\square\)

## 9. One operation, two local directional laws

Theorem 8.1 is the first exact example in FCOA-Z of a property that changes with geometric sector while the operation symbol, carrier, and distinguished right argument remain unchanged.

The rule is not introduced by a sign case table. It is forced by:

1. the old ray law (13);
2. reversible completion of the carrier;
3. the derived reflection;
4. equivariant extension of the same operation.

Therefore the local formulas

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}=T^{-1}
\quad(k>0),
\]

and

\[
R_{x_0}^{\oplus}=T
\quad(k<0)
\]

are **completion-induced sector laws** of one operation.

## 10. Left/right role asymmetry survives completion

### Proposition 10.1

For every \(k\ne0\),

\[
\boxed{x_0\oplus x_k=x_k}
\tag{16}
\]

while

\[
\boxed{x_k\oplus x_0=x_{k-\operatorname{sgn}(k)}\ne x_k.}
\tag{17}
\]

Hence the original positional noncommutativity is preserved globally across both directions.

### Proof

Equation (16) is the positive old law (12) and its reflected copy. Equation (17) is Theorem 8.1. Since \(k\ne0\), moving one step toward the origin changes the point. \(\square\)

## 11. Mixed-Sector Localization restated over reversible completion

Let

\[
X^+=\{x_k:k>0\},
\qquad
X^-=\{x_k:k<0\}.
\]

### Theorem 11.1

Suppose the positive-positive legacy operation cells are fixed and the signed extension is reflection-equivariant. Then the entire negative-negative sector is forced. Every remaining new binary base-domain/value choice is localized in

\[
\boxed{
(X^+\times X^-)
\cup
(X^-\times X^+).
}
\tag{18}
\]

### Proof

For \(x,y\in X^-\), there are unique \(x',y'\in X^+\) such that \(x=\nu x'\), \(y=\nu y'\). Reflection equivariance forces both definedness and value from the already fixed cell \((x',y')\). Mixed-sign cells are not reflections of positive-positive cells, so they remain the only unconstrained base-base reflection orbits. \(\square\)

## 12. Separation between generated geometry and mixed interaction

The results above establish two different stages.

### Stage A — forced by reversible completion

Without choosing any new mixed-sign rule, one already obtains:

- a two-sided line;
- a unique origin reflection;
- sector-dependent direction of the right-zero action;
- global radial contraction toward the root;
- a residual branch-reflection symmetry;
- localization of all further binary interaction freedom to mixed sectors.

### Stage B — still open

Only later must one choose a generator for

\[
X^+\times X^-
\quad\text{and}\quad
X^-\times X^+.
\]

Therefore the first publication need not solve the mixed-sector value law in order to establish a genuine symmetry-induced change in operation behavior.

## 13. Classical boundary

The construction “make a noninvertible system invertible by a minimal/natural extension” has substantial classical precedent in dynamical systems, and the passage from a one-sided shift to a two-sided shift is standard.

Accordingly, FCOA-Z makes **no novelty claim** for minimal reversible/natural extension itself.

The research claim to audit is narrower:

> transporting a pre-existing partial, role-sensitive FCOA operation through this reversible completion produces a sector-dependent radial law and localizes all genuinely new binary freedom to the cross-origin sectors.

This combination must be compared against partial algebras, graded algebras, associative/Jordan pairs, categories/groupoids, and local algebraic structures before priority is claimed.
