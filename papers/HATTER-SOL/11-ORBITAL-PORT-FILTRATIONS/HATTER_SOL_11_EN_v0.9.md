## Abstract

Typed arithmetic-network models compress local arithmetic structure before global optimization. This raises two distinct information questions: what is already forgotten by the local arithmetic interface, and what further information is erased only after a network architecture is imposed?

For the imaginary-quadratic HATTER interface we answer both questions at the level of shortest lattice directions. In a generic odd-discriminant world the natural symmetry action has one axial direction orbit and one conjugate oblique orbit. The magnitude-sorted pair

\[
\Pi(\alpha)=(P,Q),\qquad P\ge Q\ge0,
\]

is therefore a forgetting map from a finer intrinsic direction-orbit datum. For every strict interior pair `P>Q>0` we prove that there are exactly three canonical orbital placements,

\[
(P;\{Q,0\}),\qquad(Q;\{P,0\}),\qquad(0;\{P,Q\}),
\]

with orbit-total network states

\[
(P,Q),\qquad(Q,P),\qquad(0,P+Q).
\]

These states are pairwise distinguishable already on the two-vertex connected host. Their later loss of distinguishability depends sharply on geometry. Every even path preserves all three. In the outerplanar class the only interior collision is the mixed rank swap `(2,1)\leftrightarrow(1,2)`; the pure-oblique state never joins that collision. By contrast, if `H` is a connected even-order `d`-regular 1-factorizable host and total local capacity satisfies `P+Q\ge d`, then the exact two-channel Pareto response depends on the host only through `(n,d)` and the local capacities. Consequently the interior response-class count obeys

\[
\boxed{
3\quad(d<P),\qquad
2\quad(P\le d<P+Q),\qquad
1\quad(d=P+Q).
}
\]

At the tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral orders this fixed-host theorem lifts to the entire planar class and gives the exact trajectory `3\to2\to1` for the fiber `(4,1)` along orders `4\to6\to12`.

The graph-theoretic ingredients used in the proofs are classical. The contribution claimed here is their composition with an arithmetic symmetry quotient: intrinsic direction orbit \(\to\) folded interface \(\to\) exact Pareto network response \(\to\) geometry-controlled information loss. We also prove that the arithmetic orbit filtration does not imply a universal strongest-first activation law.

## 1. Introduction

HATTER-SOL-09 introduced world-dependent typed factor interfaces and Pareto network responses. HATTER-SOL-10 extended the construction to prime ideals and minimal principalization witnesses. In both cases a local arithmetic object is compressed before global graph optimization.

The present paper asks what that compression forgets.

The central architecture is

\[
\boxed{
\Omega\longrightarrow\Xi\longrightarrow\Pi=(P,Q)\longrightarrow Z_{\mathcal C,n}^{\Xi}.
}
\]

Here `Omega` is the intrinsic direction-orbit signature, `Xi` is its canonical orbit-total two-channel quotient, `Pi` is the magnitude-sorted HATTER interface, and `Z` is the exact connected Pareto response for a fixed host or geometry class.

Three different losses must be separated:

1. **arithmetic forgetting** — orbit placement is lost under the fold to `(P,Q)`;
2. **network forgetting** — distinct `Xi` states acquire the same optimized response;
3. **observational forgetting** — a restricted probe or scalar observable merges responses that remain distinct in the full Pareto object.

Only the first two enter the main theorem.

The graph ingredients below belong to classical factor theory: `(g,f)`-factors, factorization, edge-color reductions, 1-factorizations, multiple degree constraints, colored matching variants, and degree-sequence optimization [1--8]. No novelty is claimed for those ingredients themselves.

## 2. Arithmetic direction orbits

Let `K` be an imaginary quadratic field with discriminant `Delta` and ring of integers `O_K`. The HATTER additive step set is

\[
S_\Delta=\{\pm1,\pm F_\Delta,\pm\overline{F_\Delta}\},
\]

with duplicates removed. Passing modulo sign gives the undirected direction set

\[
D_\Delta=S_\Delta/\{\pm1\}.
\]

Let `Gamma_Delta` be generated by complex conjugation and by multiplication by units whenever that multiplication preserves `D_Delta`.

### Theorem 2.1 — orbit-fusion classification

\[
\boxed{
\#(D_\Delta/\Gamma_\Delta)=
\begin{cases}
1,&\Delta=-4,\\
1,&\Delta=-3,\\
2,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
\]

In a generic even world `Delta<-4`, `Delta=0 mod 4`, the two orbits are `[1]` and `[F]`. In a generic odd world `Delta<-3`, `Delta=1 mod 4`, with

\[
F=\frac{1+\sqrt\Delta}{2},\qquad\bar F=1-F,
\]

the two orbits are

\[
\boxed{A=\{[1]\},\qquad O=\{[F],[\bar F]\}.}
\]

At `Delta=-4` the Gaussian unit `i` fuses the two square directions. At `Delta=-3` an Eisenstein unit cycles all three hexagonal directions modulo sign. No other imaginary-quadratic field has a larger unit group. ∎

In every generic world the rational direction has absolute norm one while the second orbit lies in a strictly larger norm shell. Thus there is a canonical two-level norm-shell filtration. At the Gaussian and Eisenstein worlds the filtration collapses because all selected step directions already have norm one.

### Theorem 2.2 — no universal strongest-first activation law

In every generic world there exist arithmetic elements whose shortest HATTER representation uses only the outer norm shell while using zero capacity from the inner shell.

**Proof.** Take `alpha=mF`, `m>0`. In the generic even case its shortest representation uses only transverse `F`-steps. In the generic odd case Theorem 3.1 below gives geodesic triple `(0,m,0)`. Therefore outer-shell use can occur with zero inner-shell use. ∎

Hence a canonical orbit filtration exists, but a universal sequential activation rule does not.

## 3. Generic-odd geodesics and the fold

Assume from now on

\[
\Delta<-3,\qquad\Delta\equiv1\pmod4,
\]

and write

\[
\alpha=L+WF.
\]

Every representation by the three positive directions has the form

\[
\alpha=x+yF+z\bar F.
\]

Since `bar F=1-F`,

\[
L=x+z,\qquad W=y-z.
\]

Putting `t=z`, every integral representation is

\[
x=L-t,\qquad y=W+t,\qquad z=t,
\]

with word length

\[
f(t)=|L-t|+|W+t|+|t|.
\]

### Theorem 3.1 — median geodesic theorem

Let

\[
m=\operatorname{med}\{L,-W,0\}.
\]

Then `f(t)` has the unique minimizer `t=m`, so

\[
\boxed{g_\Delta(\alpha)=(L-m,W+m,m).}
\]

At least one coordinate is zero.

**Proof.** `f(t)` is the sum of distances from `t` to the three points `L,-W,0`. For three points the unique minimizer of the sum of absolute distances is their median. Since the median is one of the three values, one displayed coordinate vanishes. ∎

### Theorem 3.2 — geodesic folding theorem

If

\[
\Pi_\Delta(\alpha)=(P,Q),\qquad P\ge Q\ge0,
\]

is the published odd-discriminant HATTER pair, then

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{sort}_{\downarrow}\{|x|,|y|,|z|\}=(P,Q,0).
}
\]

Moreover

\[
\boxed{P+Q=|x|+|y|+|z|=\max\{|L|,|W|,|L+W|\}.}
\]

Thus `(P,Q)` is exactly the magnitude-sorted forgetting of the unique direction-labelled geodesic.

Define

\[
\boxed{\Omega_\Delta(\alpha)=\bigl(|x|;\{|y|,|z|\}\bigr)}
\]

and its orbit-total quotient

\[
\boxed{\Xi_\Delta(\alpha)=(A,O):=(|x|,|y|+|z|).}
\]

The semicolon separates the axial orbit from the unordered oblique pair. Sign changes do not affect absolute counts and conjugation only exchanges the oblique entries. All exact network theorems below concern `Xi`; no claim is made for every richer network semantics retaining the full `Omega` datum.

## 4. Exact fibers of the forgetting map

### Theorem 4.1 — complete generic-odd fiber classification

For fixed folded data `P>=Q>=0`,

\[
\boxed{
|F^{-1}(P,Q)|=
\begin{cases}
1,&P=Q=0,\\
2,&P>0,\ Q=0,\\
2,&P=Q>0,\\
3,&P>Q>0.
\end{cases}}
\]

For `P>Q>0`, the three signatures are

\[
\boxed{
\Omega_I=(P;\{Q,0\}),\qquad
\Omega_{II}=(Q;\{P,0\}),\qquad
\Omega_{III}=(0;\{P,Q\}),
}
\]

with orbit totals

\[
\boxed{
\Xi_I=(P,Q),\qquad
\Xi_{II}=(Q,P),\qquad
\Xi_{III}=(0,P+Q).
}
\]

**Proof.** Theorem 3.1 leaves at most two nonzero direction counts. Modulo exchange of the two oblique slots, an interior pair can place the two positive magnitudes in exactly three ways: `P` axial, `Q` axial, or neither axial.

All three occur explicitly. Take

\[
\alpha_I=P+QF,
\qquad
\alpha_{II}=Q+PF,
\qquad
\alpha_{III}=Q-(P+Q)F.
\]

The median formula gives, respectively,

\[
(P,Q,0),\qquad(Q,P,0),\qquad(0,-P,Q),
\]

up to overall sign and exchange of the two oblique slots. Hence the three stated orbital signatures are realized. The boundary cases follow by the same slot-counting argument. ∎

Let

\[
q=\frac{1+|\Delta|}{4}>1.
\]

For an interior pair the three placements have norms

\[
N_I=P^2+PQ+qQ^2,
\]

\[
N_{II}=Q^2+PQ+qP^2,
\]

\[
N_{III}=q(P^2+Q^2)+(2q-1)PQ.
\]

Since

\[
N_{II}-N_I=(q-1)(P^2-Q^2)>0,
\]

\[
N_{III}-N_{II}=(q-1)Q(Q+2P)>0,
\]

we have

\[
N_I<N_{II}<N_{III}.
\]

Thus `(N_Delta,Pi_Delta)` already determines `Omega`. The orbital signature is a canonical unfolding of `(P,Q)`, not an independent static invariant once norm is also retained. Its new role is operational: the network is allowed to see orbit placement before the fold discards it.

## 5. Exact orbit-total network response

Let `H=(V,E)` be a connected simple host on `n` vertices. Put the same orbit-total state

\[
\Xi=(A,O),\qquad A,O\in\mathbb N_0
\]

at every vertex.

A feasible network is a connected spanning subgraph of `H` whose used edges are typed axial or oblique. At every vertex axial degree is at most `A`, oblique degree is at most `O`, and a host edge is used at most once in total.

If `e_A,e_O` are the typed edge counts, define

\[
\boxed{B_A=nA-2e_A,\qquad B_O=nO-2e_O.}
\]

Let `R_H^Xi(A,O)` be the Pareto-minimal feasible boundary set and

\[
\boxed{Z_H^{\Xi}(A,O;X,Y)=\sum_{(B_A,B_O)\in\mathcal R_H^{\Xi}}X^{B_A}Y^{B_O}.}
\]

For a geometry class `C`, `Z_{C,n}^{Xi}` denotes the Pareto minimum over all connected simple hosts of order `n` in that class. This distinction between a fixed host and a whole geometry class is maintained throughout.

### Proposition 5.1 — exact two-node response

On the unique connected two-vertex host,

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_2^{\Xi}(A,O)=
\begin{cases}
\{(2A-2,2O),(2A,2O-2)\},&A>0,\ O>0,\\
\{(2A-2,0)\},&A>0,\ O=0,\\
\{(0,2O-2)\},&A=0,\ O>0,\\
\varnothing,&A=O=0.
\end{cases}}
\]

Connectivity forces the unique edge to be present; its type gives the displayed alternatives.

### Theorem 5.2 — operational visibility of every interior fiber

For every `P>Q>0`, the two-node responses of

\[
(P,Q),\qquad(Q,P),\qquad(0,P+Q)
\]

are pairwise distinct.

The pure state has a singleton response, while each mixed state has two Pareto points. The minimum first coordinate of the mixed responses is `2P-2` and `2Q-2`, respectively, so the mixed states differ as well. ∎

## 6. Strict one-dimensional memory

For strict one dimension the connected host on `n=2m` vertices is the path `P_{2m}`. If exactly `r` path edges are axial, then

\[
B_A=2mA-2r,
\qquad
B_O=2mO-2(2m-1-r).
\]

A capacity-one channel must occupy a matching; a capacity at least two imposes no additional local restriction on a path. Define

\[
r_{\max}(A)=
\begin{cases}
0,&A=0,\\
m,&A=1,\\
2m-1,&A\ge2,
\end{cases}
\]

and

\[
r_{\min}(O)=
\begin{cases}
2m-1,&O=0,\\
m-1,&O=1,\\
0,&O\ge2.
\end{cases}
\]

### Theorem 6.1 — exact path response

\[
\boxed{
Z_{P,2m}^{\Xi}(A,O;X,Y)
=
\sum_{r=r_{\min}(O)}^{r_{\max}(A)}
X^{2mA-2r}Y^{2mO-2(2m-1-r)}.
}
\]

Every integer in the displayed interval is realizable; all points have the same total boundary and are therefore mutually incomparable.

### Corollary 6.2 — permanent interior memory in 1D

For every `P>Q>0` and every even path order,

\[
\boxed{
Z_I^{1D}\ne Z_{II}^{1D},\qquad
Z_I^{1D}\ne Z_{III}^{1D},\qquad
Z_{II}^{1D}\ne Z_{III}^{1D}.
}
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{1D:\quad3\to3\to3\to\cdots.}
\]

## 7. Complete outerplanar classification

Let `n=2m>=4`. We use two explicit outerplanar families.

`G_m` is the outer cycle `C_{2m}` together with nested chords

\[
\{j,2m-j\},\qquad1\le j\le m-1.
\]

It has `3m-1` edges, two vertices of degree two and all other vertices of degree three.

`H_n` is the chain of triangles with edge set

\[
\{i,i+1\},\quad1\le i\le n-1,
\]

and

\[
\{i,i+2\},\quad1\le i\le n-2.
\]

It is maximal outerplanar, has `2n-3` edges and maximum degree at most four.

Every outerplanar graph has at least two vertices of degree at most two.

Define

\[
\psi_n(S)=
\begin{cases}
2,&S=3,\\
n(S-4)+6,&S\ge4,
\end{cases}
\]

and for `A>=3`

\[
\phi_n(A)=
\begin{cases}
2,&A=3,\\
n(A-4)+6,&A\ge4.
\end{cases}
\]

### Proposition 7.1 — exact pure-channel law

For every `S>=3`,

\[
\boxed{Z_{O,n}^{\Xi}(0,S;X,Y)=Y^{\psi_n(S)}.}
\]

For `S=3`, the two low-degree vertices force boundary at least two and `G_m` attains it. For `S>=4`, the edge ceiling `2n-3` gives the stated lower bound and `H_n` attains it.

### Proposition 7.2 — exact channel floor

For every `A>=3` and every other-channel capacity `O>=0`,

\[
\boxed{\min B_A=\phi_n(A).}
\]

For `A=3`, two low-degree vertices force two unused axial ports. For `A>=4`, the axial subgraph itself is outerplanar and hence has at most `2n-3` edges. The graphs `G_m` and `H_n` attain the corresponding bounds. In particular `phi_n` is strictly increasing on `A>=3`.

### Proposition 7.3 — exceptional `(2,1)` response

For every `m>=2`,

\[
\boxed{
Z_{O,2m}^{\Xi}(2,1)=Z_{O,2m}^{\Xi}(1,2)=X^2+Y^2,
}
\]

while

\[
\boxed{Z_{O,2m}^{\Xi}(0,3)=Y^2.}
\]

The total-capacity-three lower bound is two. The graph `G_m` realizes both even allocations `(0,2)` and `(2,0)` for each mixed state and `(0,2)` for the pure state.

### Theorem 7.4 — complete rank-swap classification

For every `P>Q>0` and even `n>=4`,

\[
\boxed{
Z_{O,n}^{\Xi}(P,Q)=Z_{O,n}^{\Xi}(Q,P)
\iff
(P,Q)=(2,1).
}
\]

**Proof.** If `Q>=3`, the minimum axial exponents are `phi_n(P)` and `phi_n(Q)`, which differ by strict monotonicity.

If `Q=1` or `2` and `P>=3`, the unswapped state has positive axial boundary in every outerplanar realization. In the swapped state the axial channel can be saturated on `H_n`: use a perfect matching when `Q=1` and a Hamiltonian cycle when `Q=2`. The complementary color class satisfies the capacity-`P` degree bound. The two channel edge sets partition `E(H_n)`, so their **union is the full connected host `H_n`**. HATTER connectedness applies to this union; neither color class is required to be connected separately. Thus the swapped response contains a Pareto point with zero axial boundary, whereas the unswapped response does not. The remaining case is `(2,1)`, covered by Proposition 7.3. ∎

### Theorem 7.5 — complete outerplanar interior-fiber law

For every `P>Q>0`,

\[
\boxed{
\nu_{O,n}^{\Xi}(P,Q)=
\begin{cases}
2,&(P,Q)=(2,1),\\
3,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
\]

It remains only to exclude mixed/pure collisions. Type I is separated from the pure state by positive minimum axial boundary whenever `P>=3`, and `(2,1)` is explicit. For Type II with `Q>=3`, the same argument applies. If `Q=1,2`, the minimum oblique exponent of the swapped state is `phi_n(P)`, whereas the pure state has exponent `psi_n(P+Q)`. For `P=3`, these are `2` versus `6` or `n+6`; for `P>=4`,

\[
\psi_n(P+Q)-\phi_n(P)=nQ>0.
\]

Hence no mixed state equals the pure state. ∎

## 8. Regular factorizable hosts

Let `H` be a connected simple `d`-regular graph on an even number `n` of vertices, and assume a fixed 1-factorization

\[
\boxed{E(H)=M_1\sqcup\cdots\sqcup M_d.}
\]

### Lemma 8.1 — degree-window interpolation

For `0<=l<=u<=d` and every integer `e` satisfying

\[
\frac{nl}{2}\le e\le\frac{nu}{2},
\]

there exists a spanning subgraph `F` with exactly `e` edges and

\[
l\le d_F(v)\le u
\]

for every vertex.

**Proof.** Write `e=nq/2+r`, `0<=r<n/2`. Take `q` full 1-factors and `r` edges of the next factor. Every vertex has degree `q` or `q+1`. ∎

### Theorem 8.2 — exact regular-factorizable response

Let every vertex have capacities `(A,O)` and suppose

\[
\boxed{S=A+O\ge d.}
\]

Set

\[
D=n(S-d),\qquad
L_A=n(A-d)_+,\qquad
L_O=n(O-d)_+.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_H^{\Xi}(A,O)=
\left\{(B_A,B_O):
\begin{array}{l}
B_A+B_O=D,\\
L_A\le B_A\le D-L_O,\\
B_A\equiv0\pmod2
\end{array}
\right\}.
}
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
Z_H^{\Xi}(A,O;X,Y)=
\sum_{\substack{b=L_A\\b\equiv0\ (2)}}^{D-L_O}X^bY^{D-b}.
}
\]

**Proof.** Any feasible network uses at most `nd/2` host edges, so `B_A+B_O>=D`. Channelwise edge counting gives `B_A>=L_A`, `B_O>=L_O`.

To attain every parity-compatible point on the lower-total line, use all edges of `H`. Let the axial subgraph be `F` and type the complement oblique. The local constraints become

\[
(d-O)_+\le d_F(v)\le\min(A,d).
\]

Because `A+O>=d`, this interval is nonempty. Lemma 8.1 realizes every required axial edge count. The union of the two channels is the full connected host `H`.

Any feasible point above the lower-total line is dominated by a realized point on that line: choose an even

\[
b\in
\left[
\max(L_A,D-B_O),
\min(B_A,D-L_O)
\right].
\]

The interval is nonempty by the coordinate lower bounds and `B_A+B_O>=D`. Thus no point above the line is Pareto-minimal. ∎

**Scope warning.** The hypothesis

\[
\boxed{A+O\ge d}
\]

is part of the theorem. The underfull regime `A+O<d` is not covered by `(n,d)` universality because a selected union of fewer than `d` 1-factors may be disconnected.

### Theorem 8.3 — degree-controlled orbital-port filtration

For an interior fiber `P>Q>0`, `S=P+Q`, and a connected even-order `d`-regular 1-factorizable host with `d<=S`,

\[
\boxed{
\nu_H^{\Xi}(P,Q)=
\begin{cases}
3,&d<P,\\
2,&P\le d<S,\\
1,&d=S.
\end{cases}}
\]

The two mixed fronts coincide exactly when `P<=d`, because their lower axial endpoints are `n(P-d)_+` and `n(Q-d)_+`. For `d<S`, the pure state remains only the endpoint `(0,n(S-d))`; at `d=S` all three states close to `(0,0)`. ∎

Thus

\[
\boxed{3\xrightarrow{d=P}2\xrightarrow{d=P+Q}1.}
\]

The first threshold forgets the placement of the larger folded magnitude; the second forgets the remaining mixed-versus-pure placement.

### Proposition 8.4 — underfull sufficient closure condition

If `S<d` and `H` contains a connected spanning union `J` of `S` factors from the chosen 1-factorization, then any partition of those factors into `A` axial and `O` oblique factors, `A+O=S`, gives

\[
\boxed{\mathcal R_H^{\Xi}(A,O)=\{(0,0)\}.}
\]

No converse is claimed.

## 9. Exact planar lifts

A simple planar triangulation on `n` vertices has `3n-6` edges. If it is `d`-regular,

\[
nd=6n-12,
\qquad
d=6-\frac{12}{n}.
\]

For even `n>=4`, integrality leaves exactly

\[
\boxed{(n,d)=(4,3),(6,4),(12,5).}
\]

These are the tetrahedron, octahedron and icosahedron. Each is connected and 1-factorizable.

Crucially, each also attains the **global planar edge ceiling at its order**. Therefore the fixed-host lower-bound line in Theorem 8.2 is also the geometry-class lower-bound line at `n=4,6,12`. This independent extremality step is what permits the lift from a fixed host to the whole planar class.

### Corollary 9.1 — exact Platonic planar response

For

\[
(n,d)\in\{(4,3),(6,4),(12,5)\}
\]

and `A+O>=d`, the exact planar geometry-class response equals the response in Theorem 8.2.

For an interior fiber `P>Q>0`, `S=P+Q>=d`,

\[
\boxed{
\nu_{Pl,n}^{\Xi}(P,Q)=
\begin{cases}
1,&S=d,\\
2,&S>d\text{ and }P\le d,\\
3,&S>d\text{ and }P>d.
\end{cases}}
\]

For `(P,Q)=(4,1)` this gives

\[
\boxed{
\nu_{Pl,4}^{\Xi}=3,
\qquad
\nu_{Pl,6}^{\Xi}=2,
\qquad
\nu_{Pl,12}^{\Xi}=1.
}
\]

Hence along the exact planar sequence

\[
4\to6\to12
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{3\to2\to1.}
\]

This is an exact three-scale witness, not a monotonicity claim for every intermediate order.

Larger nonregular planar hosts contain additional combinatorics. The separate Gaussian `(6,5)` repair programme is intentionally not a dependency of this paper.

## 10. Observation protocols are separate filters

A restricted search protocol can forget different information from the full Pareto response. For example, a maximal-first probe can identify a different pair of orbital states from the pair identified by full optimization on the same host.

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\text{probe-induced forgetting need not equal model-induced forgetting.}
}
\]

This is another reason not to reinterpret the degree thresholds as an intrinsic strongest-first arithmetic law.

## 11. Discussion

The results isolate three levels of information.

First, the fold

\[
\Omega\to\Xi\to(P,Q)
\]

forgets static direction placement. Second, network optimization can further identify distinct orbit-total states. Third, an observation protocol can merge responses that the full Pareto object still separates.

Host size alone does not force forgetting. A path can grow indefinitely while its local incidence ceiling remains two and all three interior states remain visible. Outerplanar graphs permit more edges but retain unavoidable low-degree vertices, so they forget only the exceptional `(2,1)` rank swap. Regular factorizable hosts reveal a clean local-degree threshold law.

Within Theorem 8.2, detailed host topology disappears from the uniform `Xi` response once `(n,d)` is fixed. But this universality has a sharp boundary: when `S<d`, the full host is no longer used and connectedness of the selected spanning factor becomes a genuine topological condition.

The paper does **not** prove a full network theory for `Omega=(a;{b,c})`, a complete planar classification, a genus theorem, universality for arbitrary regular graphs, or automatic underfull closure.

---

## 12. Conclusion

The magnitude-sorted HATTER pair `(P,Q)` can hide canonical arithmetic direction placement. In a generic odd imaginary-quadratic world, a strict interior pair is the image of exactly three intrinsic orbital states. Their orbit-total network images are

\[
(P,Q),\qquad(Q,P),\qquad(0,P+Q).
\]

They are operationally distinct on the smallest connected host. Geometry then determines how much of this information survives optimization.

Strict one dimension preserves all three at every scale. Outerplanar geometry has exactly one interior mixed collision, `(2,1)`, and no mixed/pure collision. Connected regular 1-factorizable hosts in the critical/overfull regime obey

\[
\boxed{3\xrightarrow{d=P}2\xrightarrow{d=P+Q}1.}
\]

The resulting object is not a hierarchy of stronger and weaker arithmetic ports. It is a hierarchy of **observable arithmetic memory**.

## Appendix A. Explicit Platonic 1-factorizations

For `K_4` on vertices `1,2,3,4`, take

\[
M_1=\{12,34\},\qquad M_2=\{13,24\},\qquad M_3=\{14,23\}.
\]

For the octahedron, write

\[
\mathcal O_6=K_6-M_0
\]

for one perfect matching `M_0`. A 1-factorization of `K_6` containing `M_0` leaves four perfect matchings partitioning `E(\mathcal O_6)`.

For the icosahedron use vertices

\[
N,S,u_0,\ldots,u_4,v_0,\ldots,v_4
\]

with indices modulo five and edge families

\[
N-u_i,\quad S-v_i,\quad u_i-u_{i+1},\quad v_i-v_{i+1},\quad u_i-v_i,\quad u_i-v_{i-1}.
\]

The five pairwise edge-disjoint perfect matchings
